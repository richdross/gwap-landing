Imagine somebody looking for a service. Instead of opening a list of search results, they ask an AI assistant to compare a few businesses. In a supported browsing experience, the assistant can visit web pages, inspect details, and report back with a recommendation.

The customer gets an answer. The businesses may never know which pages were considered or skipped.

That's the new question for the web: **What happens to discovery when software helps decide which sources people encounter?**

The consequences could reach publishers, creators, and local businesses. Before predicting the outcome, we need to separate two different technologies and look closely at the evidence.

## AI summaries and browser agents are different

An AI summary in search synthesizes information alongside or within a search-results experience. A browsing agent can take actions across web pages: navigate, click, collect information, and continue toward a user's goal.

OpenAI described an assistant operating its own browser when it introduced Operator in January 2025. That functionality was later integrated into ChatGPT agent. Google described agentic tasks and web-linked answers as part of its AI Mode development in 2025. Their capabilities, permissions, and safeguards differ by product and can change over time.

Sources: [OpenAI on Operator](https://openai.com/index/introducing-operator/) and [Google on AI Mode](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/google-search-ai-mode-update/).

Both developments can affect where attention goes. But an AI Overview click study is not automatically evidence about an assistant choosing destinations while browsing.

That distinction matters because it changes what we should measure.

## What the click data actually shows

Pew Research Center examined browsing behavior from U.S. adults who agreed to share data from March 2025.

Users clicked a traditional search result in **8% of Google visits that included an AI summary**, compared with **15% of visits without one**.

The comparison is significant, but the limits are just as important. These are observed visits, not percentages of traffic lost by every publisher. The analysis does not establish that the AI summary caused every difference. People may perform different kinds of searches in the two groups.

Read the [Pew Research Center findings](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/).

Google has presented an ecosystem-wide view that should also be part of the conversation. In August 2025, it reported that aggregate organic click volume from Google Search to websites had remained relatively stable year over year, with a small increase in what the company calls quality clicks.

Source: [Google's account of AI Search traffic](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/ai-search-driving-more-queries-higher-quality-clicks/).

Pew's session-level observation and Google's aggregate claims measure different things. Neither can tell you what is happening to a particular website without its analytics.

## The new gatekeeper is the selection path

Consider three hypothetical local repair companies. All three have functioning websites. Each lists services, coverage areas, contact information, and evidence of completed work.

A person asks an assistant to compare the companies.

The questions now include:

1. Can the assistant access and interpret each website?
2. Which sources does it actually inspect for this request?
3. Are the selected sources visible and verifiable to the user?
4. Does the assistant deliver links, a summary, or a recommendation without a visit to each source?

This is a hypothetical example. It is not a hidden ranking formula, a controlled test, or proof that every agent treats every business the same way.

Agentic browsing can be genuinely useful. It can save time and help people navigate complicated sites. The challenge is preserving enough visibility into the assistant's decision path to check its work.

That is why our earlier article on [the gatekeepers of AI commerce](/blog/the-new-gatekeepers-of-ai-commerce-why-agent-access-is-becoming-the-real-power-layer/) matters: access, permissions, and distribution can influence what an agent is able to do.

## Five moves for site owners right now

**Make your pages useful on their own.** State what you do, where you operate, who is responsible, when information was updated, what evidence supports a claim, and how to contact you. Visitors should understand the important facts without deciphering an animated interface.

**Publish something original.** Firsthand demonstrations, real comparisons, reporting, explanations based on actual work, and documented examples give people reasons to visit and share a page. Google emphasizes [helpful, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) and warns that mass-producing low-value AI pages can violate its policies.

**Check discoverability.** A useful page needs to be fetchable, accessible, internally linked, and eligible for indexing. Verify a valid canonical URL and a sensible mobile reading experience. Submitting a sitemap or requesting indexing does not guarantee a ranking.

**Measure the journey, not a headline.** Track search impressions, queries, clicks, indexing, and meaningful actions on your website. Compare like-for-like time periods before attributing a change to an AI feature. An unexplained dip is an investigation, not a conclusion.

**Distribute reporting in several formats.** One genuinely researched story can support a YouTube documentary segment, a linked article, a short clip, and a concise newsletter. Each format should offer standalone value. Cloning generic AI text onto dozens of pages gives audiences little reason to return.

## The GWAP Gatekeeper experiment

For *The Internet Has a New Gatekeeper*, GWAP Gang is developing a short film that combines a real question about AI navigation with our original fictional world.

The Seeker, the Watchers, and DAC are storytelling characters. Cinematic surveillance images are **fictional visual metaphors**. They do not demonstrate real-world browser behavior.

Our evidence standard is separate: when we say an assistant opened a public site and followed a link, we need a genuine, reviewable recording of that interaction. A still image or animation should never pretend to be the missing screen capture.

We are keeping that production gate in place before publication.

This is the kind of original experiment that can make technology coverage more useful: show what happened, explain what can be inferred, and say plainly what the evidence cannot establish.

## Who controls what you never see?

The web still contains information worth seeking directly. AI tools may help people reach it faster and make online tasks easier. They may also introduce a new selection layer between the person asking and the pages being consulted.

When a tool chooses the route through the internet, the most important question might be simple:

**How can we see what it skipped?**
